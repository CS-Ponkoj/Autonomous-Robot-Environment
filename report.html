Teaching a robot to drive

A small robot crosses a simulated office while cats wander around. We built and tested 13 kinds of “brains” for it. The best one now reaches the goal in 57 of 60 test drives, beats the hand-written driver, and has never hit anything.

The goal

Drive to the goal, around walls and cats, without a crash

Every drive, the robot starts somewhere in a five-room office (office, lab, corridor, storage, reception) and must reach a goal in another room within 130 seconds. In half of the test drives, four cats roam the rooms, sit in doorways and get in the way. A decision model reads the robot’s sensors and decides, ten times a second, how fast to drive and how much to turn. A built-in safety layer stops the robot if it is about to hit something; every time it has to step in counts as an “intervention”.

Rules we kept throughout: the simulated environment is never changed; every model drives through the same plug as the robot’s hand-written driver; every model is tested on exactly the same drives.

How we measure

Sixty fixed test drives, the same for every model

Each drive number fixes the start, the goal, how many cats there are and how they behave. So every model faces identical situations.

Test setDrivesUsed for
PracticeLayouts 2000 to 2009, each with 0 and with 4 cats (20)Comparing during training
40-drive checkLayouts 2000 to 2019 (40); 2010 to 2019 were never used for tuningA larger, fairer check
Held-out examLayouts 1000 to 1009, 0 and 4 cats (20)The final test, run once per finished model

Score = (goals reached − 2 × collisions) ÷ drives. 1.0 is perfect; a crash costs double. The test is latency-aware: the world keeps moving while a model thinks, so a slow model really drives worse. “All 60” means the 40-drive check plus the held-out exam.

The core idea of phase 2

A planner beside every model

The model never sees the map. A planner holds the map, plans the route every half second, and hands the model four numbers. The model combines them with its own sensors and decides the actual speed and turn. It can ignore the route, for example to wait for a cat.

The 4 route numbersMeaning
Direction (sine and cosine)Which way the route goes, 0.5 m ahead, relative to where the robot faces. Two numbers avoid a jump at ±180°.
Route known1 if the planner found a route to the goal, 0 if not.
Route lengthDistance to the goal along the route. Much longer than the straight line means a wall is in the way.

Step 1 · the saved map

The robot maps the building itself

The building never changes, so a real robot would keep a map. We let the robot build one from its own lidar on 40 mapping drives, never copying the simulator’s floor plan, which we kept only to check accuracy. The second map was built while correcting drift against the first.

Step 2 · drift correction

A map only helps if the robot knows where it is on it

Odometry drifts by a few degrees per drive. Without correction, the saved walls and the live readings no longer line up, doorways look closed, and a saved map makes driving worse, even a perfect one. Every half second the robot now matches its lidar scan to the saved walls and corrects its position.

Step 3 · text models that drive

From words to “words plus numbers”

In phase 1, text models read eight rough distances as a sentence. A doorway inside a 45° slice looked like a wall, so they never found the way. In phase 2 they read a short sentence (goal, contact, route) while all 72 lidar readings and the route enter as numbers through a small extra layer.

The first one, MiniLM, reached 0.55 on practice in its very first run: the first text model ever to reach goals. ModernBERT, DeBERTa, Laya and ModernBERT-large followed.

Step 4 · a hidden bug

The teacher and the student saw different routes

When a model learns from its own mistakes, a teacher labels what it should have done. The teacher used the planner without the map, while the model saw the route with the map. Where the two chose different ways around a wall, the labels told the model to steer against its own input. Four training runs collapsed (0.00 to 0.20) before we found it.

Share of turning moments where the teacher turns toward the model’s route

Step 5 · the cat problem

A resting cat became a permanent wall

Replaying a failed drive showed the robot knew exactly where it was, yet its route pointed 150° away from the goal. The planner kept any obstacle seen for half a second for the rest of the drive. A cat that sat briefly in a doorway closed that doorway forever.

A simple timer fixed the cats but made the robot forget real furniture missing from the map, and loop back into it. The rule that worked: forget an obstacle that is not on the map only after the robot has seen it gone (a lidar beam passes through its spot), plus five seconds.

What the gain is made of

  • Saved map + drift correction: the route no longer misleads in rooms the robot has not seen yet.
  • Matching teacher: retraining improves models instead of breaking them.
  • Planner fix: cats no longer become walls; cat drives on the exam went from 4 to 9 of 10.
  • One gentle round: models adapt to the new route; no-cat drives recovered (DT 17 → 18, ModernBERT 16 → 19 of 20).

The best model is the same small network as at the start (about 196,000 parameters). What changed is the information it gets and how it was taught.

Text models and Laya

Text models can drive now, but small specialised models still win

The best text model, ModernBERT-base, reached 53 of 60, tied the built-in driver on the exam and drove very smoothly (12 interventions in 20 exam drives). It is 10 to 15 times slower per decision than the small models.

The Laya question. To isolate Laya’s own pretraining we trained its generic counterpart, ModernBERT-large, with the identical recipe and identical training data. Laya was ahead by one drive in 20 at both stages (0.65 vs 0.60, then 0.80 vs 0.75): consistent, but within chance on 20 drives. Laya also scored below the smaller, fully trained ModernBERT-base on the exam (0.70 vs 0.80). Our reading: for driving through this interface, Laya’s pretraining adds at most a small edge.

How the work was done

A small team, strict rules, and a lot of checking

The team

  • Lead: planning, the map planner, drift correction, the cat fix, benchmarks, reviews and every decision on what to keep.
  • Text-model helper: the words-plus-numbers design, all text-model training, and the discovery of the labelling bug.
  • Route helper: route input and retraining for the small models, the cat-drive replay.
  • Two reviewers on the lab PC: read-only code reviews that caught real bugs before they cost runs.

The rules we held

  • Never change the environment; models use the same plug as the built-in driver.
  • Identical test drives for every model; each exam taken once.
  • Benchmarks taken while another heavy job or a game ran were thrown out and redone.
  • A hardware guard and memory watchdogs paused or stopped jobs before the computer struggled.
  • Every run is logged, failures included.

Bugs found by review and testing

  • Map votes counted twice and left holes in walls.
  • Drift correction ignored small errors; ghost walls after corrections.
  • The planner’s fallback brought cat walls back.
  • A test-set leak between training rounds; a silently ignored data option.
  • Teacher and model using different planners (the big one).

Problems along the way

  • Lab PC crashed three times under load; heavy work moved to the desktop.
  • Google Drive on the desktop stopped uploading for hours; found by checking the cloud copies.
  • Python libraries inside Google Drive loaded slowly; worked around with a cache warm-up.
  • Two jobs together nearly ran out of memory; watchdogs stopped them in time.

Our thoughts

What we believe now

  • Information beat model size. The biggest jumps came from better inputs (a saved map, a route that does not lie about cats), not from bigger networks.
  • Teaching signals must match what the student sees. One mismatch quietly destroyed four training runs.
  • Small, fast models are the right tool here. They drive best, decide in 2 to 5 ms and train in minutes.
  • Text models are viable drivers but not better ones. Their value may lie elsewhere, for example explaining a decision in words.
  • The current tests are nearly saturated. With 57 of 60, three timeouts with cats are all that is left; we need harder tests to tell the top models apart.
